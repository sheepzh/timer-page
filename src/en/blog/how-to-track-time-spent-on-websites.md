# How to Track Time Spent on Websites in Chrome, Edge and Firefox

You sit down to check one thing, and somehow forty minutes later you're three layers deep into a comment section. If you want to change that, the first step is measurement: a complete, automatic record of where your browsing time actually goes.

Your browser can tell you exactly this: a small extension tracks time spent on each website in the background. No manual timers, no journaling, no guesswork.

This guide shows the setup step by step, using a free and open-source tracker that keeps all data on your own device.

## Why not just check your browsing history?

Your browser history records *visits*, not *duration*. You can see that you opened a news site at 14:32, but not that you stayed for an hour. That gap makes history a poor basis for understanding habits.

A dedicated time tracker solves this by watching the active tab: while a tab stays focused and visible, the extension accumulates time for that domain. When you switch away, the clock stops. The result is a per-website duration report: the number you actually want.

## Step 1: Install a time tracking extension

For this walkthrough we use <a href="/en/install" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Time Tracker - Web Habit Builder</a>, a free, open-source extension available for Chrome, Edge and Firefox. Install it from your browser's store and tracking starts immediately; there is no account to create and no configuration required for basic use.

From the first minute after installation, every website you focus on is recorded with second-level precision.

## Step 2: See where your time goes

Open the extension's dashboard (click its icon, then choose the analysis page). You get:

- **Time per website and per day**, shown as charts and rankings
- **Trends across weeks**, so you can compare this week against your baseline
- **Time-of-day distribution**, which reveals when your attention tends to drift

A tip: look at your top five sites and ask one question for each: "was this time intentional?" Domains with a long tail of unintended visits are your first candidates for the [time limiting rules](/en/guide/basic/limit).

## Step 3: Turn data into action

Tracking alone doesn't change behavior, but it tells you exactly where a rule would help. For example, if the report shows 90 minutes per day on video sites, you can set a daily limit for that pattern. See [How to Limit Time on YouTube and Other Websites](/en/blog/limit-time-on-youtube-websites) for the walkthrough.

Because the tracker records everything locally, the numbers are yours to keep. You can [export the data](/en/guide/data) as JSON or CSV at any time, or analyze trends with the built-in [habit reports](/en/guide/basic/analysis).

## What to look for in a time tracker

Whichever tool you pick, check four things:

1. **Automatic tracking**: active-tab based, no manual timers to start and forget
2. **Local storage**: your browsing history is sensitive; it should stay on your device
3. **Multi-browser support**: Chrome, Edge and Firefox if you use more than one
4. **Exportability**: your data belongs to you, in an open format

Time Tracker - Web Habit Builder meets all four: it is MIT-licensed open source, stores data exclusively in your browser's local storage, and works across all three major browsers. If you are coming from another tracker, you can even [import your existing data](/en/guide/data) instead of starting from zero.
